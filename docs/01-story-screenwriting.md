# 01 · Story & Screenwriting

## What it is (simply)

A **screenplay** is the written blueprint of a film: what we *see* and *hear*, scene by scene, in present tense. **Story** is the deeper thing underneath — a character who wants something, faces obstacles, and changes (or fails to). Screenwriting is the craft of turning a story into scenes a camera can capture.

Key words defined:
- **Logline** — the whole film in one or two sentences: *who* wants *what*, *what stands in the way*, and *what's at stake*.
- **Beat** — one unit of change in a scene (a decision, a reveal, a reaction).
- **Scene heading (slugline)** — `INT. KITCHEN – NIGHT`: inside or outside, location, time of day.
- **Action lines** — what we see. **Dialogue** — what characters say.
- **Shot list** — the script broken into individual camera shots.

## Why it matters for AI filmmaking specifically

1. **AI makes images cheap, so story becomes the differentiator.** Festivals and clients have seen thousands of pretty AI clips. What they haven't seen is *your* story.
2. **AI video works in 5–10 second chunks.** You must think in shots and beats, not long continuous takes. Writing visually — one clear action per shot — makes generation dramatically easier.
3. **Every line of action becomes a prompt.** "She hesitates at the door" is filmable. "She feels conflicted about her past" is not — you need to show it.
4. **Dialogue drives cost.** Lip-synced dialogue is the hardest and most expensive thing to generate well. Good AI scripts use voice-over, off-screen dialogue, and visual storytelling smartly.

## Core concepts

**Beginner**
- Logline formula: *When [inciting incident], a [flawed protagonist] must [goal] before [stakes/clock], but [obstacle].*
- Three-act structure: setup → confrontation → resolution.
- Show, don't tell. One visual action per line.
- Screenplay format basics (1 page ≈ 1 minute of screen time).
- Short-film scope: 1–2 characters, 1–3 locations, one clear change.

**Intermediate**
- Want vs need; character arcs.
- Scene goals, conflict, and "turns" (every scene should change something).
- Subtext — characters rarely say what they mean.
- Visual motifs and setups/payoffs.
- Converting a script into a numbered **shot list** with durations.

**Advanced**
- Structure variants: five-beat short structure, kishōtenketsu (four-part, conflict-light), circular stories.
- Writing *for the medium*: designing scenes around what current AI does well (atmosphere, scale, surreal transitions) and avoiding what it does badly (complex hand interactions, many people talking at once, text on screen).
- Rewriting with feedback; table reads with AI voices.
- Series/episodic writing and pitch documents (bible, lookbook).

## Hands-on exercise: "The 60-second silent short"

1. Write **10 loglines** in 30 minutes using the formula above. No judging.
2. Pick the one that can be told **without dialogue** in about 60 seconds.
3. Write a 1-page script: max 2 characters, 2 locations.
4. Break it into a shot list of **8–12 shots**, each 3–8 seconds, with: shot #, shot size, what happens, sound.
5. Read it aloud (or have an AI voice read it). Cut anything that isn't a visible action.

**Done when:** someone who hasn't read your logline can watch your storyboard later and tell you what the story was.

## How AI helps at this stage

- **Brainstorming:** "Give me 20 loglines about loneliness in a megacity; make 5 of them comedies." Then *you* choose.
- **Critique, not authorship:** paste your logline and ask "What's unclear? Where are the stakes weak?" Keep the creative decisions yours — this also matters for copyright (see [08](08-legal-ethics-copyright.md)).
- **Formatting:** convert rough notes into screenplay format; check page count.
- **Shot-list conversion:** "Turn this scene into a numbered shot list with shot size, duration, and one visual action per shot."
- **Table reads:** generate quick AI voice readings to hear pacing problems.
- **Watch out for:** generic, cliché suggestions (AI tends to drift toward the average). Ask for "unexpected" options and push back.
